\section{Functional Analysis Review}

Here I review some very specific topics and results that I reviewed for my viva. 

\subsection{The First and Second Algebraic Dual Space \cite[pg. 106-107]{krey} }

The \textit{first algebraic dual space} $X^*$ of a vector space $X$ is the set of all linear functionals defined on $X.$ The \textit{second algebraic dual space} of $X$ is $X^{**}.$ There is an important relation between $X$ and $X*.$ To see this, let $x$ be a general element of $X,$ $f$ be a general element of $X^*,$ and $g$ be an element of $X^{**}.$ This $g$ is obtained by fixing $x \in X$ and setting
\begin{equation}
    g(f) =g_x(f) = f(x).
\end{equation}
Thus, to each $x \in X$ we can find a $g_x \in X^{**}.$ This relation defined the canonical mapping $J_X : X \rightarrow X^**$ by
\begin{equation}
    \langle J_x u, f \rangle_{X^{**}, X^*} = \langle f, u \rangle_{X^*, X}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Isomorphisms \cite[pg. 108]{krey} }

Given two spaces $X$ and $\tilde X$ of the same kind (both vector spaces, normed spaces, etc), we want to know if the spaces are `` essentially identical." That is, we want to know if they differ at most by the nature of their points. If so, then we may regard the spaces as identical when we care about the space structure and not about the nature of its points. 

This idea leads to the concept of an \textit{isomorphism}, which is a bijective map from $X$ to $\tilde X$ that preserves structure\footnote{For metric spaces, the structure is the metric; for a vector space, the structure is the two operations, and so on.} For example, when $X$ and $\tilde X$ are metric spaces, an isomorphism is a bijective map that preserves distances. 

This brings us back to the canonical mapping of the previous section. The canonical mapping is injective. Furthermore, it is an isomorphism from $X$ onto its image. Recall that if $X$ is isomorphic to a subset of $Y$, then $X$ is \textit{embeddable} in $Y.$ Thus, here, $X$ is embeddable in $X^{**},$ where $J_X$ is the canonical embedding. Furthermore, if $J_X$ is surjective (,and hence bijective) then $X$ can be identified with $X^{**}.$ In this case, $X$ is said to be \textit{algebraically reflexive.}

\subsection{Normed Spaces; Isopmorphism, Dual, Anti-Dual \cite[Section 2.10]{krey}}

Now let $X$ be a normed space. An isomorphism of a normed space $X$ onto a normed space $\tilde X$ is a bijective linear map $T : X \rightarrow \tilde X$ that preserves norms, i.e., $\| Tx \| = \|x\|$. In that case, $X$ and $\tilde X$ are isomorphic. From an abstract point of view, $X$ and $\tilde X$ can be taken to be identical, where points in $X$ can be `renamed' as points in $\tilde X$ by attaching a $T$ to each point. 


Recall that when $X$ is a normed space, the \textit{dual space} $X^*$ is the space of all bounded linear functionals on $X.$ Furthermore, recall that taking the dual reverses inclusions:
\begin{equation}
    V \subset W \implies W^* \subset V^*.
\end{equation}

In the complex setting, we often consider the \textit{anti-dual space}, which is the space of all bounded conjugate-linear functionals on $X.$ 

\subsection{Hilbert Spaces \cite[Section 3.1]{krey}}

 Recall that an \textit{inner product} is a map $(\cdot, \cdot) : X \times X \rightarrow \mathbb{F}$ ($\mathbb{F}$ is the field of interest, either $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C})$ that satisfies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $(x+y, z) = (x, z) + (y, z)$
    \item $(\alpha x, y ) = a (x, y)$
    \item $(x, y) = \overline{(y, x)}$
    \item $(x, x) \ge 0  \text{ and } (x, x) = 0 \iff x = 0,$ 
\end{itemize}
for every $x, y \in X$ and $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}.$ An isomorphism $T: X \rightarrow \tilde X$ between inner product spaces $X$ and $\tilde X$ is a bijective linear operator that preserves the inner product, i.e., $(Tx, Ty) = (x, y).$


Hilbert spaces are complete inner product spaces. Since the inner product induces the norm $\|x\|^2 = (x, x),$ a Hilbert space is also a Banach space (one-way implication). 

\subsection{Total Orthonormal Sets and Seperable Hilbert Spaces \cite[Section 3.6]{krey}}

We begin with a metric space $X.$ A set $M \subset X$ is \textit{dense} in $X$ if $\overline M = X.$ The set $X$ is said to be \textit{separable} if it has a countable subset which is dense in $X$

Now we turn to normed spaces. A \textit{total} set in a normed space $X$ is a subset $M \subset X$ whose span is dense in $X.$ Accordingly, if $X$ is an inner product space, then an orthonormal set in $X$ that is total in $X$ is called a total orthonormal set in $X.$ Every nontrivial Hilbert space has a total orthonormal set. Furthermore, every total orthonormal set in a Hilbert space has the same cardinality, which is called the \textit{Hilbert dimension} of the space. In fact, two Hilbert spaces are isomorphic if and only if they have the same Hilbert dimension. Thus, fir each Hilbert dimension, there is just one abstract real Hilbert space and just one abstract complex Hilbert space. In other words, two abstract Hilbert spaces over the same field are distinguished only by their Hilbert dimension. 

\subsection{Riesz Representation \cite[Section 3.8]{krey} \cite[Section 2.4]{PatrickNote}}\label{Riesz}

We begin by recalling Riesz's theorem:

\begin{theorem}[Riesz Theorem]\footnote{See \cite[3.8-1]{krey}.}
    Every bounded linear functional $f$ on a Hilbert space $H$ can be represented in terms of the inner product
    \begin{equation}
    f(x) = (x, z),
\end{equation}
where $x \in H$ and $z$ is uniquely determined by $f$ and has norm $\|z\| = \|f\|$ (ordering follows from the fact that $f$ must be linear in $x$). This defines the \textit{Riesz map} $R : H^* \rightarrow H,$ which is an isometric isomorphism (conjugate-linear, bijective, norm-preserving) that maps $f \mapsto z.$ 
\end{theorem}


Let $X$ and $Y$ be two vector spaces over the same field $\mathbb{F}.$ A \textit{sesquilinear} form $h$ is a mapping $h : X \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{F}$ such that for all $x, x_1, x_2 \in X,$ for all $y, y_1, y_2 \in Y,$ and for all scalars $\alpha, \beta,$ we have
\begin{itemize}
    \item $h(x_1 + x_2, y) = h(x_1, y) + h(x_2, y)$
    \item $h(x, y_1 + y_2) = h(x, y_1) + h(x, y_2)$
    \item $h(\alpha x, y) = \alpha h(x, y)$
    \item $h(x, \beta y) = \overline{\beta} h(x, y).$
\end{itemize}
Thus, $h$ is linear in its first argument and conjugate-linear in its second. When $X$ and $Y$ are real, the last condition reads $h(x, \beta y) = \beta h(x, y)$ and $h$ is called \textit{bilinear} (linear in both arguments). 

From the Riesz theorem, we can obtain a general representation of sesquilinear forms on Hilbert spaces:
\begin{theorem}[Riesz Representation for Sesquilinear Forms]
    Let $H_1$ and $H_2$ be two Hilbert spaces and let $h : H_1 \times H_2 \rightarrow \mathbb{F}$ be a bounded sesquilinear form. Then $h$ has the representation 
\begin{equation}
    h(x, y) = (Sx, y),
\end{equation}
where $S : H_1 \rightarrow H_2$ is a bounded linear operator (ordering follows from the fact that $h$ must be linear in $x$ and conjugate-linear in $y$). This $S$ is uniquely determined by $h$ and has norm $\| S \| = \| h \|.$
\end{theorem}




\subsection{Orthogonal Projections \cite[Section 3.3, 9.5]{krey}}

$Y$ is a closed subpace of the metric space $X$ iff it has the following property: if $x_n \in Y$ and $x_n \rightarrow x$ then $x \in Y$ (that is, $Y$ contains all its limit points, or $Y = \overline Y$). 

Let $Y$ be aby closed subspace of the Hilbert space $H.$ Then we can write 
\begin{equation}
    H = Y  \oplus Y^\perp.
\end{equation}
In other words, for every $x \in H$ we can \textbf{uniquely} write $x = y + z$ where $y \in Y$ and $z \in Y^\perp.$ With this, we define $y = x-z$ to be the \textit{orthogonal projection} of $x$ onto $Y$ (it is the component of $x$ that is perpendicular to $Y$). 

This defined the mapping $P : H \rightarrow Y$ by $x\mapsto y = Px$ which is the orthogonal projection of $H$ onto $Y.$ This $P$ is also idempotent, that is, $P^2 = P.$ Also, $P$ maps $Y$ to itself and $P$ maps $Y^\perp$ to $\{0\}.$ Thus, $Y^\perp$ is the nullspace of $P.$ 

\subsection{Unbounded Operators on Hilbert Spaces}

% For unbounded operators, we need to be extra careful when considering domains, adjoints, and extensions. For example, we need to distinguish between a symmetric operator and a self-adjoint operator. A symmetric operator is necessarily symmetric but also must have the same domain as its adjoint. 

For unbounded operators, particular care must be taken when considering domains, adjoints, and extensions. In particular, it is important to distinguish between symmetric and self-adjoint operators. A densely defined operator $A: D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H$ is symmetric if $(Ax, y) = (x, Ay)$ for each $x, y \in D(A)$. However, self-adjointness requires the stronger condition $A=A^*$. Thus, a symmetric operator is self-adjoint if and only if its domain coincides with that of its adjoint, i.e., $\mathcal{D}(A)=\mathcal{D}(A^*)$.


\subsection{Hilbert Adjoint of Unbounded Operators}

Let $A : D(A) \subset H_1 \rightarrow H_2,$ where $D(A)$ is densely defined in the Hilbert space $H_1.$ Fix a $w \in H_2$ and consider 
\begin{equation}
    F_w : D(A) \rightarrow \mathbb{C}, \qquad F_w(v) = (Av, w)_{H_2},
\end{equation}
which is linear as a function of $v \in D(A).$  

We set $D(A^*)$ to be the set of all $w$ for which the corresponding $F_w$ is bounded with respect to the $H_1$ norm. Thus, if $w \in D(A^*)$ then the map $v \mapsto (Av, w)_{H_2}$ is a bounded linear functional on the dense subspace $D(A) \subset H_1.$ 

Since the bounded linear functional $F_w$ is densely defined, it can be uniquely extended to a bounded linear functional on all of $H_1,$ $\tilde F_w : H_1 \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ (BLT theorem, why density matters). Then, on $H_1$ we can apply the Riesz Representation Theorem to say that there is a unique $u \in H_1$ such that
\begin{equation}
    \tilde F_w(v) = (v, u)_{H_1} \qquad \forall v \in H_1. 
\end{equation}
Thus, restricting back to $D(A),$ we have that 
\begin{equation}
    (Av, w)_{H_2} = (v, u)_{H_1} \qquad \forall v \in D(A). 
\end{equation}
Using this, the Hilbert adjoint of $A$ is the unique $A^*$ such that $A^*w = u.$

Before moving on, we state some properties of the Hilbert adjoint (assuming $A$ is densely defined):

\begin{itemize}
    \item  $A^*$ is closed. 
    \item $A^*$ is densely defined if and only if $A$ is closable ($A$ has a closed extension). 
    \item If $A$ is closable, then its closure is $A^{**}.$
    \item $R(A)^\perp = N(A^*).$
    \item If $A$ is closed, then $R(A)^\perp = N(A)$ (infinite-dimensional analogue of the Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra)
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relating Hilbert and Banach Adjoints}

Let $A : D(A)\subset H_1 \rightarrow H_2$ be a densely defined operator, where $H_1$ and $H_2$ are Hilbert spaces. Using the Riesz Representation Theorem for each Hilbert space, we can define the Riesz maps:\footnote{Note that in \Cref{Riesz}, the Riesz map was taken to map $H^*$ to $H.$ This is a matter of taste. If you would like, you can think of the `Riesz map' of this section as the `inverse Riesz map' of \Cref{Riesz}.}
\begin{equation}
    R_i : H_i \rightarrow H_i^*, \qquad \langle R_i w, v \rangle_{H_i^*, H_i} = (v, w)_{H_i}.
\end{equation}

Let's fix a $w \in D(A^*) \subset H_2$ and consider $R_2 w \in H_2^*.$ By the definition of the Banach adjoint, 
\begin{equation}
    \langle A^\dagger R_2 w , v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1} = \langle R_2 w, Av \rangle_{H_2^*, H_2}.
\end{equation}
Then, by the definition of the Riesz map,
\begin{equation}
    \langle A^\dagger R_2 w , v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1} = \langle R_2 w, Av \rangle_{H_2^*, H_2} = (Av, w)_{H_2}.
\end{equation}
By the definition of the Hilbert adjoint,  $(Av, w)_{H_2} = (v, A^*w)_{H_1}.$ Thus, 
 \begin{equation}
    \langle A^\dagger R_2 w , v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1} = (v, A^*w)_{H_1}.
\end{equation}
Applying the Riesz map $R_1,$ we have that
\begin{equation}
    (v, A^*w)_{H_1} = \langle R_1 A^* w, v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1}. 
\end{equation}
Thus, 
\begin{equation}
     \langle A^\dagger R_2 w , v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1} = \langle R_1 A^* w, v \rangle_{H_1^*, H_1}
\end{equation}
and we have that 
\begin{equation}
    A^\dagger R_2 = R_1 A^*
\end{equation}
or
\begin{equation}
    A^* = R_1^{-1} A^\dagger R_2.
\end{equation}

Furthermore, we have that when $w \in D(A^*),$ the map $v \mapsto (Av, w)$ is bounded for every $v \in D(A)$. Using this $R_2$ Riesz map, this is equivalent to saying that $\langle R_2 w, Av \rangle_{H_2^*, H_2}$ is bounded, or that $R_2 w$ is in $D(A^\dagger)$ (take $f = R_2 w$ in \Cref{Domain banach}). Thus, we have that
\begin{equation}
    D(A^\dagger) = R_2 D(A^*).
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{What Happens in Finite Dimensions?}

In finite dimensions, every space is closed. Thus, $D(A) = H_1.$ Furthermore, every linear operator on a finite-dimensional normed space in bounded \cite[Theorem 2.7-8]{krey}. Thus, $D(A^*) = H_2$ and $D(A^\dagger) = H_2^*.$ 

Let's take $H_1 = \mathbb{C}^n$ and $H_2 = \mathbb{C}^m.$ Recall that linear operators on finite-dimensional
spaces can be represented in terms of matrices. Thus, we can think of $A$ as an $m \times n$ matrix.\footnote{For more details, see \cite[Section 2.9]{krey}.} Furthermore, we can think of linear functionals that map from $\mathbb{C}^n$ to $ \ mathbb {C} $ as $1 \times n$ matrices (row vectors). 

Consider the functional $f \in (\mathbb{C}^{m})^*.$ Let's say that the row vector that represents $f$ is $c^T.$ Then
\begin{equation}
    (A^\dagger f)(v) = f(Av) = c^T (Av) = (A^T c)^T v.
\end{equation}
Thus, the vector representation of $A^\dagger f$ is $A^T c.$
In this way, the Banach adjoint can be thought of as the matrix transpose. 

Similarly, let $B$ be the matrix representation of $A*.$ We have that $(Av, w) = (Av)^T \overline y = v^T A^T \overline y.$ Furthermore, $(v, A^*w) = x^T \overline B \overline y.$ Since $(Av, w) = (v, A^*w),$ we have that $B = \overline A ^T.$ In this way, the Hilbert adjoint can be thought of as the conjugate transpose of the matrix representation of $A$.\footnote{The discussion of this section is discussed in more detail in \cite[Section 4.5]{krey}.}

\section{Selected Spectral Theory Results from the Thesis}



\subsection{What Does a Zero Injection Modulus Mean?}

Recall the definition of the injection modulus of a closed and densely defined $A: D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H,$ 
\begin{equation} \label{inject}
    \sigma_{\inf}(A) = \inf\{ \|Ax\|_H : x \in D(A), \|x\|_H = 1\}.
\end{equation}
If $\sigma_{\inf}(A) = c > 0$ then $\|Ax\| \ge c \|x\|$ for every $x \in D(A).$ Thus, $Ax = 0$ implies that $x=0.$ Hence, a positive injection modulus indicates that the operator is injective.

In finite dimensions, the unit sphere is compact. Thus, the minimum of $\|Ax\|$ on the unit sphere is attained at the smallest singular value of $A.$ However, in infinite dimensions, the infimum need not be attained at any $x \in D(A).$ This gap between finite and infinite dimensions is what allows an infinite-dimensional operator to be injective but have a zero injection modulus. For example, consider the operator $Ae_n = \frac{1}{n} e_{n+1},$ which is a bounded operator on $H = \ell^2(\mathbb{N}.$ This operator is injective but has a zero injection modulus because $\|Ae_n\| = \frac{1}{n} \rightarrow 0$ as $n$ increases.


\subsection{Injection Modulus and Types of Spectral Points}


If $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is in the point spectrum, then $A-zI$ is not injective. Thus, $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = 0.$
Furthermore, if $z$ is in the continuous spectrum of $A$ then $R(A-zI)$ is dense in $H.$ Thus $\overline{R(A-zI)} = H$ and $N(A^*-\overline z I) = \{0\}.$ Assume that $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = c > 0.$ Then, by definition, $\|(A-zI) x \| \ge C \|x\|.$ Take a sequence $(x_n) \subset D(A)$ and define $y_n \defeq (A-zI) x_n.$ Suppose that $y_n \rightarrow y.$ Since $(y_n)$ is a Cauchy sequence, we have that $\| y_n - y_m \| = \| (A-zI) (x_n - x_m) \| \ge  C \| x_n - x_m \|$ for sufficently large $m, n.$ Thus, $(x_n)$ is  Cauchy sequence and $x_n \rightarrow x.$ Since $(A-zI)$ is closed, we know that $x_n \in D(A),$ $x_n \rightarrow x,$ and $(A-zI) x_n \rightarrow y$ implies that $x \in D(A)$ and $(A-zI)x = y.$ Thus, $y = R(A-zI).$ However, this would mean that $R(A-zI) = \overline{R(A-zI)} = H$ which is a contradiction ($z$ in continuous spectrum). Thus, if $z$ is in the continuous spectrum, we must have that $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = 0.$ 

Notice that the converse result is not true. That is, $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI) = 0$ does not imply that $z$ is in the point of continuous spectrum. This is because $\sigma_{\inf}(A-zI)=0$ could mean that $z$ is in the residual spectrum. To see this, consider the operator $Ae_n = \frac{1}{n} e_{n+1},$ which is a bounded operator on $H = \ell^2(\mathbb{N}.$ This operator is injective but has a zero injection modulus ($\|(A-zI)e_n\| = \|Ae_n\| = \frac{1}{n} \rightarrow 0$ as $n$ increases). Notice that $(Ax, e_1) = 0$ because $Ax = x_1 e_2 + (x_2/2) e_3 + \dots.$ Thus, $e_1 \in R(A)^\perp = N(A^*).$ Hence, $\overline{R(A)} \ne H,$ implying that $z=0$ is in the residual spectrum of $A.$

To account for this mismatch, we introduce the injection modulus of the adjoint operator, $\sigma_{\inf}(A^* - \overline zI).$ When $z$ is in the residual spectrum, $N(A^* - \overline z I)$ is nontrivial. Thus, $A^* - \overline z I$ is not injective and  $\sigma_{\inf}(A^* - \overline zI) = 0.$


\subsection{Why Closed Operators?}

Suppose that $A : D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H$ is not closed. Then, there exists a sequence $(x_n) \subset D(A),$ $x \in H,$ and $y \in H$ such that $Ax_n \rightarrow y$ and $x_n \rightarrow x$ but $x \notin D(A)$ and $Ax \ne y.$ Suppose (for sake of contradiction) that $z \notin \Sp{A}.$ Then $A-zI$ has a bounded inverse. Since $(A-zI)x_n \rightarrow y - zx,$ we have that $x_n \rightarrow (A-zI)^{-1}(y-zx).$ Thus, $x = (A-zI)^{-1}(y-zx)$ or $(A-zI) x = y - zx.$ However, this implies that $x \in D(A)$ and $Ax=y,$ which is a contradiction. Thus, if $A$ is not closed, every $z \in \mathbb{C}$ must be in $\Sp{A}.$ That is why we only study the spectrum of closed operators!

\subsection{Why is the Spectrum Closed?}

First, we recall the crucial result from \cite{Nick}:
\begin{theorem}[Invertibility and Perturbation of Closed Operators {\cite[Theorem 4.1]{Nick}} {\cite[Exercise 1.17]{Matt}}]\label{first stable}
    Let $A:D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H$ be a closed and densely defined operator such that $0 \notin \Sp{A}.$ Let $B$ be a bounded operator on $H$ such that $\|B\|\| A^{-1} \| < 1.$ Then $0 \notin \Sp{A+B}$ and 
    \begin{equation}
        \| (A+B)^{-1}\| \le \frac{\|A^{-1}\|}{1 - \|B\|||A^{-1}\|}.
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
    Recall that for a bounded operator $C,$ if $\|C\| < 1 $ then, by Neumann series, $I-C$ is invertible and $\| (I-C)^{-1} \| \le (1 - \|C\|)^{-1}$ \cite[Page 30]{kato}. In this setting, set $C = -BA^{-1}.$ Then $\|C^{-1}\| \le \|B\|\|A^{-1} \| < 1.$ Setting $S = (I-C)^{-1},$ Neumann series tells us that $S = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} C^n.$ Thus, by geometric series,
    \begin{equation} \label{S eqn}
        \|S\| \le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \|C\|^n = \frac{1}{1-\|C\|} \le \frac{1}{1 - \|B\|\|A^{-1}\|}.
    \end{equation}
Now, let $D = A^{-1}S.$ Notice that $D$ is bounded and 
\begin{equation}
    (A+B)D = (I + BA^{-1})S = (I+BA^{-1})(I - C)^{-1} = (I+BA^{-1})(I+BA^{-1})^{-1} = I.
\end{equation}
Similarly, 
\begin{equation}
   D(A+B) = A^{-1}S(A+B) =  A^{-1}S(I+BA^{-1})A = I.
\end{equation}
Thus, $D$ is the bounded inverse of $A+B.$ Furthermore, by \Cref{S eqn},
\begin{equation}
    \|(A+B)^{-1} \| = \|D\| \le \|A^{-1}\|\|S\|\le \frac{\|A^{-1}\|}{1 - \|B\|\|A^{-1}\|},
\end{equation}
as desired!
\end{proof}

Using this, we can show that the spectrum of a closed and densely defined operator is closed. To do so, we first consider $z \notin \Sp{A}.$ Then, we consider a $\tilde z$ such that $|z-\tilde z| < \| (T-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1}.$ Then,
\begin{equation}
    T - \tilde z I = T - zI + (z - \tilde z) I \defeq T-zI + S.
\end{equation}
Notice that this $S$ is a valid $B$ in \Cref{first stable}. Thus, $T-zI + S$ has a bounded inverse and $\tilde z \notin \Sp{A}.$ This tells us that around each $z \notin \Sp{A}$ there is an open ball $|z-\tilde z| < \| (T-zI)^{-1}\|^{-1}$ that is also outside of the spectrum. Thus, $\mathbb{C} \setminus \Sp{A}$ is open, and so $\Sp{A}$ is closed. 


\subsection{Resolvent Norm and the Distance to the Spectrum}

Let $z \in \Sp{A},$ then $\gamma(z, A) \defeq \| (A-zI)^{-1} \|^{-1} = 0.$ Recall (from the thesis) that $\gamma(w, A)$ is $1$-Lipschitz. Thus, 
\begin{equation}
     | \gamma(w, A) - \gamma(z, A)  =  \gamma(w, A)  \le | w - z |.
\end{equation}
Taking the infimum, we find that
\begin{equation}
    \gamma(w, A) \le \operatorname{dist}(w, \Sp{A}).
\end{equation}


Now, assume that $A$ is a normal operator. Then, we have that
\begin{theorem}[Inverse Resolvent Norm of Normal Operator {\cite[Exercise 1.8]{Matt}}]
    \begin{equation}
        \gamma(w, A) = \operatorname{dist}(w, \Sp{A}).
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Take $z \notin \Sp{A}$ ($z \in \Sp{A}$ case is trivial) and let $T = A-zI.$ Notice that $T$ is normal and that $\Sp{T} = \Sp{A} - z.$ Thus, $\operatorname{dist}(0, \Sp{T}) = \operatorname{dist}(z, \Sp{A}).$ Thus, we can equivalently show that $\|T^{-1}\| = \operatorname{dist}(0, \Sp{T}).$ With this in mind, we will rename $T$ as $A$ and show that $\|A^{-1}\| = \operatorname{dist}(0, \Sp{A}).$ 

Notice that
\begin{equation} \label{factor}
    \Sp{A^{-1}} \setminus \{0\} = \{ z^{-1} : z \in \Sp{A} \}.
\end{equation}
To see this, notice that for $w \ne 0,$
\begin{equation}\label{decomp}
    A^{-1} - w I = w( w^{-1} I - A)A^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Assume that $w^{-1} \notin \Sp{A}.$ Then $A-w^{-1}I$ is boundedly invertible. Thus, we can invert both sides of \Cref{decomp} and write
\begin{equation}
    (A^{-1} - wI)^{-1} = \frac{-1}{w} A (A-w^{-1} I)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Since $A = (A-w^{-1}I) + w^{-1} I,$ we have that
\begin{equation}\label{decomp2}
    (A^{-1} - wI)^{-1} = -\frac{1}{w} [ (A-w^{-1} I) + w^{-1} I] (A-w^{-1} I)^{-1} = -\frac{1}{w} [ I + w^{-1}(A-w^{-1}I)^{-1}].
\end{equation}
Since the right-hand-side of \Cref{decomp2} is bounded, $(A^{-1} - wI)^{-1}$ is bounded. Thus, if $w^{-1} \notin \Sp{A},$ then $w \notin \Sp{A^{-1}}.$ Taking the contrapositive, we have that if $w \in \Sp{A^{-1}},$ then $w^{-1} \notin \Sp{A}.$
Now, assume that $w \notin \Sp{A^{-1}}.$ Then $A^{-1} - wI$ is boundedly invertible. Since 
\begin{equation}
    (A-w^{-1}I) = -w^{-1}(A^{-1} - wI) A,
\end{equation}
we have that 
\begin{equation}\label{decomp3}
    (A-w^{-1}I)^{-1} = -w A^{-1} (A^{-1} - wI)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
Since the right-hand-side of \eqref{decomp3} is bounded, $w^{-1} \notin \Sp{A}.$ Taking, the contrapositive, we have that if $w^{-1} \in \Sp{A},$ then $w \in \Sp{A^{-1}}.$

Recall that for a bounded normal operator $B$, 
\begin{equation}
    \| B \| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{B}} |z|.
\end{equation}
Thus, we have that 
\begin{equation}
    \|A^{-1} \| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A^{-1}}} |z|.
\end{equation}
Since $\| A^{-1} \| > 0$ (the inverse cannot be zero operator), we have that
\begin{equation}
    \|A^{-1} \| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A^{-1}}} |z| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A^{-1}} \setminus \{0\}} |z|.
\end{equation}
Using \Cref{factor}, we have that
\begin{equation}
    \|A^{-1} \| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A^{-1}}} |z| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A^{-1}} \setminus \{0\}} |z| = \sup_{z \in \Sp{A}} \frac{1}{|z|} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{dist}(0, \Sp{A})},
\end{equation}
which is the desired result.
% Assume that $A^{-1} - wI$ is boundedly invertible. Thus, $R(A^{-1} - wI) = H$ (by definition of bounded invertibility) and $N(A^{-1} - wI) = \{0\}$ (because injectivity is necessary condition for invertibility).  Furthermore, using \Cref{factor} we have that $R(w^{-1} I - A) = H.$ Consider an $x \in N(w^{-1}I - A).$ By applying $A^{-1} - wI$ to $Ax,$ it becomes clear that $Ax \in N(A^{-1} - wI).$ However, since $A$ is invertible, this $x$ must be zero

\end{proof}



\subsection{Relating Spectrum of $A$ and $A^*$}

\begin{theorem}[Relating $\Sp{A}$ and $\Sp{A^*}$]
    Let $A$ be closed and densely defined. We have that
    \begin{equation}
        \Sp{A^*} = \{ \overline z : z \in \Sp{A}\}
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    We first assume that $z \notin \Sp{A}.$ Then $B \defeq (A-zI)^{-1}$ is bounded. Let $v = B^* y$ for some $y \in H.$ For every $u \in D(A)$ we have that 
\begin{equation}
    ((A-zI)u,v) = ((A-zI)u, B^*y) = (B(A-zI)u, y) = (u, y).
\end{equation}
Thus, $(A-zI)^* v = y$ and $v \in D(A^*).$ In particular, we have found that for any $y\in H$ there is a $v \in D(A^*)$ for which $(A-zI)^* v = y.$ Thus, $(A-zI)^*$ is surjective. Furthermore, since $R(A-zI) = H,$ we know that $N((A-zI)^*) = \{0\}.$ Thus, $(A-zI)^*$ is injective. As a result, $(A-zI)^*$ is bijective and has inverse $B^*.$ Since $B$ is bounded, $B^*$ is bounded. Thus, $(A-zI)^*$ is boundedly invertible and $\overline z \notin \Sp{A^*}.$

For the other direction, recall that since $A$ is closed, $A = A^{**}.$ Using our previous argument, we have that if $\overline z \notin \Sp{A^*},$ then $z \notin \Sp{A^{**})}.$ 

Combining these two results, we have that $z \notin \Sp{A}$ if and only if $\overline z \notin \Sp{A^*}.$ Thus, $z \in \Sp{A}$ if and only if $\overline z \in \Sp{A^*}.$
\end{proof}

\subsection{Equivalent Pseudospectra Defintions}

Recall the following result.

\begin{theorem}
    Let $A:D(A) \subset H \rightarrow H$ be a closed and densely defind operator. We have the following equivalence:
    \begin{equation}
        \Spe{A} \defeq \operatorname{Cl}\left( \left\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) < \epsilon  \right\} \right) =  \operatorname{Cl} \left( \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\|_{\mathcal{B}(H)} < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E) \right)
    \end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
    First, suppose that $z \in \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\|_{\mathcal{B}(H)} < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E).$ For ease of notation, let $B \defeq A-zI.$ Suppose that $\|B^{-1}\|^{-1} \le 1/\epsilon,$ which implies that $z \notin \Sp{A}.$ Then, $\|EB^{-1} \| \le \|E\|\|B^{-1} \| = 1.$ Since $B+E (!+EB^{-1})B$ and $\|EB^{-1}\| < 1,$ we can apply a Neumann series asrgument  to infer that $I + EB^{-1}$ is boundedly invertible. Thus, $B+E$ is the product of two boundedly invertible operators, making it a boundedly invertible operator itself. As a result, we have that $B + E = (A+E)-zI$ is boundedly invertible and that $z \notin \Sp{A+E}.$ However, this is a contradiction. Thus, we must have that $\|B^{-1}\| > 1/\epsilon,$ which implies that $z \in \Spe{A}.$

    For the other direction, suppose that $z \notin \Sp{A},$ which implies that $B$ is boundedly invertible. Further suppose that $\|B^{-1}\| > 1/\epsilon,$ i.e., that $z \in \Spe{A}.$ Recall that (proved below), 
    \begin{equation}
        \|B^{-1} \|^{-1} = \min \{ \sigma_{\inf}(B), \sigma_{\inf}(B^*) \}.
    \end{equation}
    Using the fact that $\sigma_{\inf}(B) = \inf_{\|x\| = 1} \|Bx\| / \|x\|$ and by setting $y = Bx,$ we have that 
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{\inf}(B) = \inf_{y} \frac{\|y\|}{ \|B^{-1}y \|} = \left(  \sup_y \frac{\|B^{-1} y \|}{ \| y \| }\right)^{-1} = \frac{1}{\|B^{-1} \|}.
    \end{equation}
    Similarly, 
    \begin{equation}
        \sigma_{\inf}(B^*) = \frac{1}{\|(B^*)^{-1}\|} = \frac{1}{\|(B^{-1})^*\|}.
    \end{equation}
    Since $\|B\| = \|B^*\|,$ we have that $\sigma_{\inf}(B) = \sigma_{\inf}(B^*) = \|B^{-1}\|^{-1} < \epsilon.$ Since $\sigma_{\inf}(B) < \epsilon,$ we know that there must be some unit vector $x$ such that $\|Bx\| < \epsilon.$\footnote{Suppose this were not the case. Then $\|Bx\| \ge \epsilon$ for every admissable $x.$ Since $\sigma_{\inf}(B)$ is the greatest lower bound for $\|Bx\|,$ and $\epsilon$ is a lower bound for $\|Bx\|,$ we would have that $\sigma_{\inf}(B) \ge \epsilon.$ This is a contradiction. Thus, there must be some $x$ for which $\|Bx\| < \epsilon.$ }
    Set $y = (A-zI)x$ for such $x.$ Then $\|y\| < \epsilon.$ Furthermore, set our canddiate $E$ so that $Ex = -(u, x)y$ for any $u \in H.$ Then, $Ex = -y$ and $\|E \| = y.$\footnote{Recall that $\|E\| = \sup_{\|u\|=1} \|Eu\|.$ Notice that $\|Eu\| = |(u, x)|\|y\| \le \|u\| \|x\| \|y\| = \|y\|.$ Now, take $u=x$ and find that, since $\|E\|$ is the supremum over any admissable $u,$ $\|E\| \le \|Ex\| = \|y\|.$ Thus, $\|E\| = \|y\|.$} 
    Thus, $E$ is a valid perturbation. Since $(A-zI)x=y$ and $Ex=-y,$ we have that
    \begin{equation}
        (A+E-zI)x = (A-zI)x + Ex = y-y=0.
    \end{equation}
    Since $x \ne 0,$ we can conclude that $z$ is an eigenvalue of $A+E.$ Thus, $z \in \Sp{A+E}$ for our chosen $E.$ As a result, $z \in \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\|_{\mathcal{B}(H)} < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E).$

    Overall, we have shown that 
    \begin{equation}
        \left\{ z \in \mathbb{C} : \gamma(z, A) < \epsilon  \right\} = \bigcup_{E \in \mathcal{B}(H), \|E\|_{\mathcal{B}(H)} < \epsilon} \operatorname{Sp}(A+E).
    \end{equation}
    Taking the closure, we get the desired result. 
    

\end{proof}
